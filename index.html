Manifold learning, unrolled

Real data often looks high-dimensional but really lives on a much simpler surface, curled up inside that bigger space. Manifold learning finds the surface and lays it flat. Before any maths, unroll one yourself.

Lesson 1 of 6

Near in space isn't near on the surface

A manifold is a shape that looks flat when you zoom in close enough, the way a field looks flat even though the Earth is round. The Swiss roll is a 2-D sheet sitting in 3-D space: its intrinsic dimension is 2 even though every point has 3 coordinates.

The manifold hypothesis says lots of real data works this way. Photos of a face turning have thousands of pixel values, but only a few things actually vary (angle and lighting), so the photos lie near a low-dimensional surface.

The catch is distance. The straight line between two points can cut through empty space between layers. What we care about is the geodesic: the shortest route that stays on the surface.

Try this

  1. Press Show me a tricky pair. The two points are close through the air, but on different layers.
  2. Compare the dashed straight line with the coral path that crawls around the roll.
  3. Turn on Show neighbour graph. The path only ever hops between nearby points. Short hops can't jump between layers, so adding them up follows the surface.
  4. Now click two points that are close together on the same layer. Notice that all three distances agree: up close, the roll really is flat.
Check yourself: why can't we just measure geodesics directly?

We never get the surface, only a cloud of sample points. The geodesic has to be estimated from the samples, and the neighbour-graph path is the simplest way to do it. The quality of that estimate depends on how densely the surface is sampled and on how you choose “neighbours”.

Check yourself: what does “intrinsic dimension 2” mean for an ant on the roll?

The ant can only move in two independent directions: along the roll and across it. Two numbers fully describe where it is, even though an outside observer uses three.

Lesson 2 of 6

Classical MDS: a map from a table of distances

Suppose all you have is a table of the distances between ten cities, with no coordinates. Can you draw the map? Classical multidimensional scaling (MDS) can, and it's the engine inside Isomap, so it's worth knowing well.

  1. Square every distance to get the matrix D(2).
  2. Double-centre it to turn distances into dot products between centred points:
B = −½ J D(2) J,   J = I − (1/n) 11⊤
  1. Take the eigenvectors of B with the largest eigenvalues. Each is one axis of the map.
  2. Coordinates are Y = V2 Λ2½, the top two eigenvectors scaled by the square roots of their eigenvalues.

The eigenvalues tell you how many dimensions you need. A flat map has two big ones and the rest near zero.

Try this

  1. Drag a city on the left map. The right map is rebuilt from the distance table alone, and it follows.
  2. Turn off Line up with my map. The shape is right, but it may be rotated or mirrored. Distances can't tell you which way is north.
  3. Raise Measurement error. Now no flat map fits the table exactly: negative eigenvalues appear (coral), and MDS returns the best compromise.
  4. Then look at the Swiss roll below. MDS trusts straight-line distances, so it squashes the layers on top of each other.

MDS is a linear method. On Euclidean distances it gives the same answer as PCA, so it can rotate and project the data but never bend it. To unroll it, we need distances measured along the sheet. That's lesson 3.

Check yourself: why does MDS square the distances first?

Squared distances expand into dot products: ‖x−y‖² = x·x + y·y − 2x·y. Double-centring cancels the x·x and y·y parts and leaves the Gram matrix of dot products, which eigendecomposition can factor into coordinates.

Check yourself: what would three large eigenvalues mean?

That no flat map can hold the distances. The points need three dimensions, as cities would if the distances came from a globe at continental scale.

Lesson 3 of 6

Isomap: MDS with distances along the surface

Isomap combines lessons 1 and 2. Swap straight-line distances for estimated geodesics, and MDS will flatten the sheet instead of squashing it.

  1. Connect each point to its k nearest neighbours, weighting each edge by its length.
  2. Find the shortest path through this graph between every pair of points (Dijkstra's algorithm). These are the geodesic estimates.
  3. Run classical MDS on that table of path lengths.

Everything hinges on k. Too small and the graph breaks into islands with no paths between them. Too large and an edge jumps between layers. One such short-circuit is enough to collapse the distances and fold the map.

Try this

  1. At k = 8 the colours on the right run smoothly along one axis: the roll is unrolled into a long rectangle.
  2. Slide k up slowly past 12. Watch for coral edges on the left, then for the map folding on the right.
  3. Slide k down to 3 or 4 and watch for the graph splitting into pieces.
  4. Raise Noise. The layers get fuzzier, so short-circuits appear at smaller k. Noise is Isomap's weak spot.
  5. Click any point in either view to see it and its neighbours in both.
Check yourself: why does one bad edge do so much damage?

Shortest paths reuse it. Every pair of points on the two layers it joins can now take the shortcut, so thousands of distances shrink at once, and MDS faithfully draws those layers on top of each other.

Check yourself: Isomap preserves global or local structure?

Global. It tries to keep every pairwise geodesic distance, so far-apart points stay far apart. That makes it good at the overall shape, and fragile when the graph is wrong.

Lesson 4 of 6

LLE: describe each point by its neighbours, then keep the description

Locally linear embedding never computes long distances at all. It relies on the idea from lesson 1 that every small patch is nearly flat, so a point can be rebuilt as a weighted mix of its neighbours.

  1. Find each point's k nearest neighbours.
  2. Find the weights that best rebuild each point from its neighbours, with weights summing to 1:
minW Σi ‖ xi − Σj wij xj ‖²
  1. Freeze the weights and find 2-D points yi that the same recipe rebuilds best. This becomes an eigenvector problem on M = (I−W)⊤(I−W). Take the eigenvectors with the smallest non-zero eigenvalues.

The weights don't change if you rotate, scale or shift a patch, so they capture its shape and nothing else. That's why they survive the trip down to 2-D.

Try this

  1. Click any point. The bars show its recipe: how much of each neighbour it takes to rebuild it. The ring in the 3-D view is the rebuilt point.
  2. Find the same point on the right: it sits among the same neighbours, mixed by the same recipe.
  3. Push Regularisation to the top. Every weight drifts towards 1/k, each point becomes a plain average, and the map loses detail.
  4. Raise k past 20. Neighbourhoods get big enough to curve, the "flat patch" assumption breaks, and the map warps.

Recipe for the selected point

Check yourself: why must the weights sum to 1?

So the recipe doesn't depend on where the patch sits. Shift every point by the same amount and a sum-to-one mix shifts with it, so the weights describe shape, not position.

Check yourself: why throw away the very smallest eigenvector?

The constant vector (every yi equal) rebuilds perfectly and has eigenvalue 0, but it collapses everything to one spot. It carries no information, so LLE uses the next ones.

Lesson 5 of 6

t-SNE: keep the neighbours, let distances go

t-SNE changes the goal. Instead of preserving distances, it preserves who is near whom, written as probabilities.

  1. In the original space, each point i picks neighbours with a Gaussian: pj|i ∝ exp(−‖xi−xj‖² / 2σi²). Each σi is tuned so the point has about perplexity effective neighbours.
  2. In 2-D, use a heavy-tailed Student-t instead: qij ∝ (1 + ‖yi−yj‖²)−1.
  3. Move the 2-D points by gradient descent to make the two match, minimising KL(P‖Q).

KL divergence punishes splitting up true neighbours heavily but barely cares about where far-away points land. So t-SNE is trustworthy about local groups and unreliable about the sizes of clusters and the gaps between them.

Try this

  1. Press Play. For the first 100 steps P is exaggerated ×4, which pulls clusters together fast. Then the map relaxes.
  2. Look at cluster C (yellow). In 10-D it's four times as spread out as A, yet on the map it's about the same size. t-SNE evens out density.
  3. Set perplexity to 5 and restart: clusters shatter into fragments. Try 60: smoother, more global.
  4. Click a point. Rings mark its 10 true nearest neighbours from the original 10-D space. Are they still close?
  5. Switch the data to the Swiss roll. t-SNE keeps neighbourhoods but often tears the sheet into strips: great for clusters, not for unrolling.

Why a heavy tail? Drag the distance.

Check yourself: is the gap between two t-SNE clusters meaningful?

Not much. The heavy tail lets dissimilar points sit far apart without much cost, so gaps get exaggerated and their sizes vary from run to run. Trust which points cluster together; don't measure between clusters.

Check yourself: what is perplexity, intuitively?

A smooth count of neighbours. Perplexity 30 means each point's Gaussian is widened or narrowed until it effectively spreads its attention over about 30 others. Dense regions get small σ, sparse ones big σ.

All four on the same data

Now compare. Each map is scored by neighbours kept: for every point, the share of its 10 true nearest neighbours that are also among its 10 nearest on the map. For the roll and the S-curve, "true" means measured along the sheet; for the clusters, in the original 10-D.

Try this

  1. Start with the Swiss roll: MDS overlaps layers, Isomap unrolls best, LLE unrolls with some squeezing, t-SNE keeps patches but tears.
  2. Pick the roll with a hole. Isomap has to route its paths around the gap, while LLE never measures across it. Does the hole keep its shape in each map?
  3. Pick the clusters. There is no sheet to unroll here, and t-SNE's separation wins.

Where to go next

UMAP follows t-SNE's neighbour-preserving idea with a different graph construction, and is often faster with a little more global structure. Laplacian eigenmaps and diffusion maps are close cousins of LLE built on the graph Laplacian. For the original papers, see Tenenbaum, de Silva & Langford (2000) for Isomap, Roweis & Saul (2000) for LLE, and van der Maaten & Hinton (2008) for t-SNE.
